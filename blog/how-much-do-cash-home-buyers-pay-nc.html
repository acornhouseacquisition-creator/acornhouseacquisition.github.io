The first question almost every homeowner asks me is: "How much will you actually pay for my house?" It's the right question. Most "we buy houses" websites avoid it. This article answers it plainly, with the same math cash buyers use, so you can judge any offer you get, including ours.

The Short Answer

Most cash buyers who repair and resell houses offer roughly 60% to 85% of what your home would be worth fully repaired. Where your house lands in that range depends mostly on one thing: how much work it needs.

How Cash Buyers Calculate an Offer (the 70% Rule)

Most investors start from a rule of thumb called the 70% rule:

Maximum offer = (After-Repair Value × 70%) − Repair Costs

  • After-repair value (ARV) is what your house would sell for on the open market once it's fully fixed up, based on recent comparable sales nearby.
  • Repair costs are the buyer's estimate of everything needed to get it there: roof, HVAC, kitchen, flooring, paint, and so on.
  • The 30% covers what the buyer spends to resell the house (agent commissions, closing costs), the months of taxes, insurance and loan interest while they renovate, and their profit.

Some buyers go higher than 70% on houses that need little work or in markets where homes resell quickly. Others go lower. But if an offer is far below this formula, ask why.

What Raises or Lowers Your Offer

  • Condition. This matters most. Every dollar of repairs usually comes straight off the offer, so an accurate repair estimate is important. Ask the buyer for theirs.
  • Comparable sales. The after-repair value comes from recent sales of similar homes nearby. If the buyer's comps are older or from a weaker neighborhood, your offer will be low.
  • How fast homes resell in your area. In areas where homes sell quickly, buyers have lower holding costs and can pay more.
  • Liens, back taxes or title problems. These don't always lower the price, but they have to be paid off at closing, which lowers what you walk away with.
  • Your timeline. Needing to close in 7 days versus 60 days rarely changes a fair buyer's price much. It mostly changes how many buyers can do it.

When a Cash Offer Is (and Isn't) Worth It

A cash sale usually makes sense when:

  • The house needs major repairs you can't pay for or don't want to manage.
  • You're behind on payments and have a foreclosure date coming. See our guide on how to stop foreclosure in North Carolina.
  • You inherited a house and the family wants to settle the estate without a renovation.
  • You're a landlord with a damaged rental or problem tenants.
  • You need a guaranteed closing date for a move, a divorce or a job.
  • The house already sat on the market without selling. See why houses aren't selling in NC.

Listing is usually better when:

  • The house is in good condition and needs little or no work.
  • You have time to wait for the right buyer.
  • You can pay for repairs and don't mind managing them.

How to Spot a Bad Cash Offer

  • They won't show their math. Ask for the after-repair value and repair estimate they used.
  • They lower the price late. Some buyers offer high to win the contract, then cut the price after an "inspection" once you're committed. Ask directly if and when the price can change.
  • Tiny earnest money. A deposit of a few hundred dollars means the buyer can walk away at little cost.
  • No proof of funds. A real cash buyer can show they have the money.
  • Surprise fees. Make sure the offer says who pays closing costs, and that nothing will be deducted from your price at closing.
  • No licensed closing attorney. In North Carolina, a licensed closing attorney handles the closing and the deed. Don't sign over a deed any other way.

The simplest protection is to get at least two offers and compare them line by line.
