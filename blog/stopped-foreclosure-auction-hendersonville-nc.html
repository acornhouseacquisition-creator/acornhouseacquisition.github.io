The Story: A Postcard, an Auction Date and a Deadline

Stephanie called us after getting one of our postcards in the mail. By then the family was in foreclosure, and the auction date had already been set.

We drove out, walked through the house with her, and talked about what she wanted. Then we made a cash offer. There was no pressure to sign that day. This is a family home and a big decision, and she needed time to think it over.

Here's the house as we first saw it. It was solid, but dated: original kitchen cabinets and flooring, and a bathroom ceiling that needed work. That's exactly the kind of house we buy as-is.

After a follow-up call a few days later, she decided to accept. We went back out to the house, signed the purchase agreement together, and opened the closing with the closing attorney right away. With an auction on the calendar, every day counted.

Stephanie told us she had already talked to three other buyers who weren't able to get it done. We didn't want to be number four.

The Problem Nobody Expected: An Expired License

Title work was moving. The payoff was being ordered. Then we hit a problem that had nothing to do with the house.

To sign a deed in North Carolina, each seller signs in front of a notary, and the notary has to confirm who they are. Normally that's a quick look at a driver's license. But one of the sellers had an expired license and no other photo ID.

Renewing wasn't a quick fix either. In North Carolina the new plastic card is mailed out, and it can take weeks to arrive. We didn't have weeks. The auction was set for Monday morning.

How we solved it: North Carolina notary law allows a notary to identify a signer through a credible witness. This is a person who personally knows the signer, and who the notary either knows or can identify with valid photo ID. The witness confirms the signer's identity under oath. We found a notary willing to handle it properly with a credible witness, and the documents were signed and notarized.

It's a legitimate option, but not every notary is comfortable with it, and it takes planning. It was one of the most stressful parts of the whole deal.

Closing on Friday, Auction on Monday

We closed on a Friday. The foreclosure auction was scheduled for the following Monday at 10 a.m. The lender was paid off through the closing, the auction didn't happen, and the family avoided a completed foreclosure.

5 Lessons If You're Facing a Foreclosure Auction

1. Call early, even if you're not sure yet

Stephanie took time to decide, and that was fine because she called us with some runway left. A week or two before an auction gives you options. A day or two often doesn't.

2. Check your ID today

If any owner on the deed has an expired driver's license or no photo ID, start fixing it now. A replacement card can take weeks to arrive. It's the kind of problem that seems small until it's the only thing standing between you and the closing table.

3. Know that you can still stop it

In North Carolina, paying off the loan before the foreclosure sale stops it. Even after the sale there's a 10-day upset bid period, and paying off the debt before it ends can still stop the foreclosure. Read our full guide on how to stop foreclosure in North Carolina.

4. Ask every buyer how they'll actually close

Three buyers before us couldn't finish the deal. Ask who the closing attorney is, whether they have the cash, and how fast they've closed before. A buyer who can't close in time is worse than no buyer at all, because they use up days you don't have.

5. Don't sign anything you don't understand

Take the time you need, ask questions, and talk to an attorney or a HUD-approved housing counselor if you want a second opinion. A good buyer won't rush you into a decision. See how cash offers are calculated so you can judge any offer you get.
