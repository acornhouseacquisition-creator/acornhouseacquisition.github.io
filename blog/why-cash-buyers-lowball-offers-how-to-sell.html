Why Are Cash Buyers Lowballing Me? (And How to Sell the Right Way)

Daniel Ghinda
Acquisition Manager, Acorn House Acquisition  ·  October 5, 2026  ·  8 min read

You call a few “we buy houses” companies, and the offers come back far below what you expected. Some feel insulting. One or two might even drop after you sign. It’s natural to wonder if everyone is trying to take advantage of you.

Some are. Most low offers, though, come down to math the buyer never explains. This article walks through that math, shows you how to spot a real lowball, and explains how to sell the right way, whether that’s to a cash buyer or not.

Why Cash Offers Are Lower Than Market Value

A cash buyer who renovates and resells is pricing in everything it costs to turn your house into one that sells at full price. Most use a version of the 70% rule:

Offer ≈ (After-Repair Value × 70%) − Repair Costs

The 30% isn’t all profit. It covers the buyer’s closing costs on both ends, agent commissions when they resell, months of taxes, insurance, utilities and loan interest during the renovation, and the risk of surprises behind the walls. What’s left is their profit.

For example, a house worth $300,000 fully updated that needs $40,000 of work comes out around $170,000 by that formula. If you were expecting $260,000, that feels like a lowball, but it may be a normal investor number. We break this down with real numbers in how much cash home buyers really pay.

When It Really Is a Lowball: 6 Reasons

1. The buyer is a wholesaler, not the end buyer

Many “cash buyers” never plan to buy your house. They put it under contract and sell that contract to another investor for a fee. Their fee comes out of your price, so their offer has to be lower than a real investor’s. Ask directly: “Will you be the one closing and taking title?”

2. Their repair estimate is padded

A $15,000 job estimated at $45,000 takes $30,000 off your offer. Ask for the repair estimate line by line.

3. They used the wrong comps

The after-repair value should come from recent sales of similar homes close by. Comps from a weaker neighborhood, or from a year ago, pull your number down.

4. They plan to renegotiate later

Some buyers offer high to get you under contract, then cut the price after an “inspection” once you’ve stopped talking to anyone else. Ask before you sign: “When, and why, could this price change?”

5. They think you’re desperate

If you mention a foreclosure date or a deadline, some buyers lower the number because they assume you have no choice. Getting more than one offer fixes that.

6. They didn’t see the house

An offer made from a phone call is a guess, and guesses get padded for risk. Let a serious buyer walk through it.

How to Sell the Right Way

  1. Know your after-repair value. Look at recent sales of updated homes near you, or ask a local agent for a free market analysis.
  2. Get a real repair estimate. One contractor walk-through tells you whether a buyer’s numbers are honest.
  3. Get at least three offers. Compare them line by line: price, who pays closing costs, closing date, and when the price can change.
  4. Ask every buyer the same questions. Are you the end buyer? Can you show proof of funds? Who is the closing attorney or title company? How have you closed recently?
  5. Read the contract. Look at the inspection period, what happens if the buyer backs out, and who pays which costs. Have an attorney review it if anything is unclear.
  6. Compare against listing. If the house is in good shape and you have time, listing with an agent often nets more. A cash sale wins when speed, certainty or condition matter more than top dollar.
